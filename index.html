The Insight

The distortion you see is caused by the relative change in depth (Z) between the camera and different parts of the face. When close to the subject, the distance from camera to nose is significantly shorter than to the ears, creating an unnatural magnification effect.

🔬

The Science

Perspective projection follows the formula:

x = f·X/Z     y = f·Y/Z

A smaller Z (depth) results in larger x,y coordinates on the image plane. At close range, the nose (smaller Z) appears magnified while the ears "shrink away." By stepping back and zooming in (increasing focal length f), we increase Z uniformly across the face, approximating orthographic projection where depth differences become negligible.

✨

What This Proves

This demonstrates that perspective projection is a "depth-dependent magnification tool." Close-ups exaggerate depth differences, creating distortion, while telephoto shots from a distance treat the face as nearly a flat plane, preserving natural proportions. The key isn't the lens—it's the camera-to-subject distance.

The Insight

This exercise demonstrates how vanishing points relate to your position in the world. The "flattened" telephoto look versus the "deep" wide-angle look is all about where vanishing points fall in your image.

🔬

The Science

Telephoto Compression (Far + Zoomed): Standing far away and zooming in moves you toward orthographic projection. Parallel lines (fence edges, building sides) converge less sharply because vanishing points move toward infinity. The distance between foreground and background appears compressed—much smaller than actual physical distance.

Wide Angle Depth (Close + Wide): When physically close, vanishing points are near the scene. Parallel lines converge steeply, creating strong 3D depth because the Z coordinate changes rapidly relative to your position—the hallmark of true perspective projection.

✨

What This Proves

The difference isn't the lens itself—it's the camera distance to the scene. Telephoto compression is really "distance compression." The rate of change in depth (Z) relative to total distance determines whether we perceive compressed or exaggerated space.

Perspective vs Orthographic

The Insight

Drawing red lines on furniture edges proves which projection model is being used, based on non-accidental properties. This isn't just about optics—it's about how we perceive and reconstruct 3D structure from 2D images.

🔬

The Science

Perspective Image: When you extend the straight edges of furniture (notebook, charger, pen), red lines converge at a vanishing point. This confirms the camera records rays meeting at a single "camera center"—the definition of perspective projection.

Orthographic/Cropped Image: With heavy zoom or center crop of a wide shot, red lines stay nearly parallel. You're looking at a tiny visual angle where convergence is minimized, mimicking the "parallel ray" straw camera model.

Generic View Assumption: We infer 3D structure because 2D lines follow these predictable geometric rules. Our visual system assumes we're not viewing from special "accidental" alignments, so converging/parallel lines reveal depth relationships.

✨

What This Proves

The red line test is a simple but powerful tool. It shows that perspective projection dominates real photography, while orthographic projection emerges only as an approximation when viewing small angular regions. Understanding which model applies helps us reason about depth, scale, and spatial relationships from flat images.
